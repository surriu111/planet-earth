Australia's Trade Geography: From Country to Place

The question

How does the physical geography of my Sydney site shape local economic opportunities and constraints, and to what extent does it help explain Australia's national export structure?

The data

I used Australia's 2024 export-by-destination data from Harvard University's Atlas of Economic Complexity, using the HS92 classification and gross export values in current US dollars. The Atlas draws on UN Comtrade goods trade data and reconciles exporter- and importer-reported values using reliability-weighted estimates. Its website permits reuse and adaptation with appropriate citation of the Atlas and underlying sources, although it does not specify a named Creative Commons licence.

For boundaries, I used Natural Earth's Admin 0 - Countries, version 5.1.1, at 1:110m scale. Natural Earth states: "All versions of Natural Earth raster + vector map data found on this website are in the public domain." These boundaries may be republished without permission.

The method

I uploaded both datasets to ArcGIS Online and performed a one-to-one left join, matching Natural Earth's NAME with the Atlas table's Name. I chose a left join to retain countries without matched trade data.

Of 177 polygons, 156 had nonblank export values and 21 remained blank. The original trade table contained 218 rows, suggesting approximately 62 unmatched trade records, assuming unique identifiers and no original blank values.

These are possible identifier mismatches, not evidence of zero trade. I found clear examples of identifier mismatches. The Atlas records Australia's exports to the Democratic Republic of the Congo ($5,254,450), the Republic of the Congo ($3,152,296), and Equatorial Guinea ($3,186,914). However, Natural Earth labels these polygons as "Dem. Rep. Congo," "Congo," and "Eq. Guinea." Because the join matched country names exactly, these records remained unmatched despite having nonzero trade values. This demonstrates that blank areas on the map do not necessarily indicate zero trade.

I mapped Gross_Export using five Natural Breaks classes and added three physical locations in Western Australia.

The evidence

What I found

One connection holds: Australia's large iron ore exports are closely linked to its mineral resources, especially in the Pilbara region of Western Australia. This contradicted my initial guess that wheat was Australia's largest export.

However, this relationship becomes less clear at the scale of my Sydney site. Although Sydney's natural harbour provides advantages for shipping and trade, Australia's major iron ore production is concentrated far from Sydney. My three mapped points are all located in Western Australia. This shows that a country's export basket may reflect its natural resources, but national trade statistics cannot reveal where economic activities actually take place.

What this does not show

I am using country-level gross export values to stand for the geography of Australia's trade, and this might fail because export values are assigned to entire destination countries, although imports may enter through specific ports and subsequently move elsewhere. The map does not show where goods are ultimately consumed or where their value is created. Meanwhile, my three points identify only selected production and shipping facilities, omitting the wider supply chain.
