When green does not mean habitat

The question

How do the pattern, scale, and density of green surfaces across my Sydney site affect what NDVI identifies as vegetation, and what is the green actually made of?

The data

I used Landsat 9 imagery from the public Multispectral Landsat service in ArcGIS Living Atlas. The sensor is Operational Land Imager. Landsat has a spatial resolution of 30 m per pixel, so each NDVI value represents an area of about 900 m² rather than an individual tree or patch of grass. The scene I used was acquired on 11 October 2024 with 0.18% cloud cover. The Landsat service is public and token-free.

The vegetation index used was NDVI. It is calculated from Landsat Band 4, the red band, and Band 5, the near-infrared band: NDVI = (NIR - Red) / (NIR + Red). Healthy vegetation tends to absorb red light and strongly reflect near-infrared light, producing a higher NDVI value.

The method

I added the Multispectral Landsat layer from Esri Living Atlas in ArcGIS Online and zoomed to my Sydney site. I first used the NDVI Raw processing template to read the actual index values at selected pixels. I then switched the same Landsat layer to NDVI Colorized to visualize the spatial pattern of vegetation using the default green-to-brown color ramp. Before looking at the results, I identified three green places for comparison: Bondi Beach, the Royal Botanic Garden, and Centennial Park, and then recorded one NDVI value at each location. In Landsat Explorer, I used the vegetation-index mask tool and set the lower threshold to 0.5, so pixels with NDVI values of 0.5 or higher were highlighted as “green.” I chose 0.5 as a simple test threshold for relatively strong vegetation signals, then checked which managed and less-managed surfaces were included.

The evidence

What I found

Before looking at the data, I predicted NDVI values of about 0.5 for Bondi Beach and 0.8 for both the Royal Botanic Garden and Centennial Park. The values I read were approximately 0.25 at Bondi Beach, 0.69 at the Royal Botanic Garden, and 0.30 at the sampled point in Centennial Park. I was more wrong about the size of the differences than the general ordering, and the Centennial Park result also showed how strongly the value depends on the exact pixel selected within a large park.

One high-index place at my site that is not necessarily high-quality habitat is the Royal Sydney Golf Course, where I recorded an NDVI of approximately 0.71. The green surface there is mainly maintained turf and landscaped vegetation, showing that a high vegetation index can reflect intensive management rather than ecological complexity.

What this does not show

I am using NDVI to stand for ecological value, and it might fail because managed lawns and planted urban vegetation at Sydney can produce high NDVI values even when they do not provide the same habitat structure as less-managed or coastal environments.

This map therefore does not show that the greenest places have the highest biodiversity or habitat quality. NDVI measures reflected light associated with vegetation, not species richness, ecological connectivity, or whether organisms can complete their life cycles there.
